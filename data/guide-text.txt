Southern Yiddish dialects, divided into CY (Central, or “Polish”; includes Hasidic Hungarian Yiddish, a.k.a. HHY) and SEY (Southeastern, or “Ukrainian”), feature a phonemic distinction between two different types of stressed  אָ, ע, י, ו [o, e, i, u], where NEY (Northeastern, or “Litvish”) does not; for example, the graphic doublet בין ‘(I) am’ and  בין ‘bee’ are respectively pronounced /bɪn/ and /bi:n/ in CY, but are both /bin/ in NEY.  This distinction is referred to as “length” for historical reasons, although the difference significantly involves quality and, in SEY, doesn’t involve length at all. For you nerdy linguist types: a vowel is short if it was short in the contributing language and occurred in a closed syllable (closed by 1 consonant in Hebrew, or 2 consonants for non-Hebrew words). For those of us without this specialized knowledge, though, length needs to be learned consciously because this distinction is not reflected in the Yiddish alphabet, nor in Standard Yiddish transliteration.

Vowels in unstressed words (e.g. סע = עס, מע, זשע) are never long, and usually reduced to /ə/.
Length distinction for [e, u, i] is neutralized before [ng, nk], though the precise realization seems to differ by variety and the linguist describing it. In the database, they are consistently marked as short for convenience.
This guide does NOT cover אַ vs ײַ (i.e. short vs. long [a]), as this is immediately apparent graphically. It also does not cover the more limited CY phenomenon of אַ representing /aː/ in certain words borrowed from German, e.g. פּלאַן.
A number of words have been imported from modern German that have long /oː/ in German. Their nativization into Yiddish varies by subdialect and the individual word, and can range from simply short /ɔ/ to long /oː/ or even long /uː/. These are marked in the database in the “Notes” column. Examples include מיליאָן, (דער) טאָן, מאָדע.
In CY, long [o] has three different realizations:
Diphthongized /uːə/ when preceding [d, t, n, l, r] in a closed syllable, e.g. ראָד /ruːəd/, באָרד /buːərt/, מאָל /muːəl/. The “offglide schwa” can also be heard in long [i] preceding [r]. However, this schwa is not found in all CY, it is notably absent in HHY.
“Shortened” /u/ before labials and velars, e.g. זקן, געגראָבן, בחור, שלאָפֿן (regionally, שטראָפֿן is an exception, with /uː/).
Plain long /uː/ everywhere else, e.g. דאָ /duː/, כּשר /kuːsher/, שאָדן /shuːdn/.
Words of newer vintage with [u] are often pronounced as /u/, and not as /ɪ/ or /i/: שטורעם (/shturəm/), גרופּע (/grupə/),  פּובליקום (/publɪkum/). These words are marked as such in the database.
Long [e] does not appear before [kh]. Additionally, some parts of SEY have no length distinction in the sequence [er]: װער (CY /veɪ̯ər/, SEY /vɛɪ̯r/ or /vɛr/).
Examples of vowel lowering before [r, kh]: קורץ (CY /kɔrts/, SEY /kɛrts/), קירצער (/kɛrtsər/), פּוך (CY /pɔkh/, SEY /pɛkh/), ליכט (/lɛkht/).
Vowel lowering has gone further in some parts of CY, where /ɛr/ — whether from underlying יר or ער — is pronounced /ar/ (same for /ɛkh/), e.g. װירט /vɛrt/ or /vart/, ליכט /lɛkht/ or /lakht/.  The database contains some 40 examples; see items marked “short {u, i} + {r, kh} = lowered” in the “Other grouping” column. Lowering does not apply to words of newer vintage, e.g. װיכטיק /vikhtɪk/ or /vɪkhtɪk/, שטורעם /shturem/.
